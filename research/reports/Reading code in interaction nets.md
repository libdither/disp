# The reader opens binders; copying dominates cost

**Is self-reflection done through a quote-like process?** Not by the reader. A quote turns a whole term into data that no longer runs. It is [a function on syntax, defined outside the calculus](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D16FE1FAB38CD06AD831C845156E10D5/S0956796800020062a.pdf/theoretical-pearls-self-interpretation-in-lambda-calculus.pdf#page=2), and [no λ-term can compute it](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D16FE1FAB38CD06AD831C845156E10D5/S0956796800020062a.pdf/theoretical-pearls-self-interpretation-in-lambda-calculus.pdf#page=2). Measured: a read in the net looks at one node and builds the same 17 agents whatever the size of the function read. (The net is the λ-calculus in `research/linear-ic-net/nets/`, run as an interaction net: a graph of nodes, called agents, that rewrites only where two agents meet. `ρ`, its one reflection primitive, puts a reader agent `R` on a value.) *Inference:* two things are still quote-like. The freezing is done by the evaluation order, not by the reader: a function body stays inert code until the call, so every function is already its own quote. And the five-argument "view" that the reader builds is shaped like one layer of Mogensen's quote (each node of a term written as a function that picks the argument for its own kind), made when asked for. That view is the big λ seen in the net's step-through player.

**Do trees fix this?** No, they move it. Tree calculus (Barry Jay's calculus in which every program is a binary tree) needs no quote only for programs that are already normal forms (terms with nothing left to rewrite): there ["the programs are already data structures, so that no pre-processing is required"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=18). The net's function bodies already have that property. Trees do one thing better: the choice between kinds of node is a rewrite rule, so no view is built. They do one thing worse: a function written with λ is first compiled to combinators (variable-free terms) outside the calculus, and after that the binder ["becomes indistinguishable from a star abstraction λ∗x.M or combinator"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=12) (star abstraction is Jay's name for that compile step). Measured in this project: random 320-node terms are 455 agents as nets and about 2,400 to 2,600 nodes as trees. And trees keep the cost that matters most here: in tree rewriting ["a single computation step may take an arbitrarily long time"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=138), since one step can copy a whole subtree. *Inference:* the gain is small and takes a case rule, not trees. The loss is the binder and the size.

**Is the reader just lazy bracket abstraction?** Not in this net. Bracket abstraction is the compile step that removes a variable from a term by threading it through routing combinators such as S and K. It has been done lazily, as a reader, once: in Jay's λSF-calculus (λ-calculus plus the operators S and F, where `F` is his read operator) a read of a function compiles one binder, ["namely, the innermost one"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=5), and reading all the way down leaves a combinator that ["is much bigger than the original term"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=6). The net's read is a different operation. By its rule table, one rewrite relabels the function as opened, the body is not walked or changed, and the binder survives. *Inference:* the standard name for this is opening a binder: apply the function to a fresh marker and get the body back with the marker in it. The "lazy" intuition holds in one sense only. Both designs make a body readable one node at a time, with no pass over the whole term.

**What other mechanisms let a graph inspect itself?** Inside interaction nets, eleven searches found no published system in which a net reads another net's code and gets back data it can branch on. The parts that exist are Lafont's copyable "code" of a net, his menu and selector (a case), one read-back done by agents (read-back: turning a net back into a term), agents that carry a net as a sealed value, wildcard rules, native case agents, and reference agents that stand for a whole definition. Outside the net there is read-back by the implementer, token machines that walk a graph without changing it, rules that are themselves graphs, nested graphs copied as one unit, and Petri nets that keep a second copy of themselves as data. Other calculi add the quote with a self-interpreter, Jay's factorisation, reading a function by running it, types that mark which code is readable, fexprs (procedures handed their operands unevaluated), towers of interpreters, the meta-level of the rewriting language Maude, and a reflective process calculus. The tables below say what each reads, what a read costs and what it cannot see. *Inference:* by the measurements, the mechanism that would change this net most is none of the readers. It is holding unchanged code by reference, so that copying or erasing a function is one step.

How to read the rest. A linked statement is what the linked source says. *Measured* means a count from this session's runs of the net's reducer (`net.py`, on programs from `check.py`) or a figure from the net's design notes (`DESIGN.md`, same folder). *Inference:* marks reasoning, by the research notes or by this report, that no source states. It covers the rest of its paragraph or table cell; a linked phrase inside it is still what the source says. Nothing was built or changed in the net during this research. Every change discussed below is an option, not a result.

## A read costs about 2,070 rewrites, and the view is 21 of them

Everything in this section is measured. The unit is the rewrite: one rule firing where two agents meet.

The net keeps a function's body as inert code agents: `lam` for a function, `app` for an application, `var` for a variable use. Nothing inside a body rewrites before the call. Evaluation is weak and call by value: arguments are run first, and nothing runs under a binder. `T` (thaw) is the agent that walks code and turns it into running applications. `ρ c` puts the reader `R` on the value `c`. When `R` meets the value it builds a view: a function of five arguments ("arms"), one per kind of value, that calls the arm for its own kind. In λ notation:

| the reader meets | the view it builds |
|---|---|
| a function (`R ⋈ lam`) | `λk1…k5. k1 k2 k3 k4 k5 F`, where F is the same binder and body, relabelled as an opened function |
| an opened function (`R ⋈ Lam`) | `λk1…k5. k2 k1 k3 k4 k5 L`, where L is the function closed again |
| an application in code (`R ⋈ app`) | `λk1…k5. k3 k1 k2 k4 k5 f a`, with f and a its two parts |
| a variable use holding a captured value (`R ⋈ var`) | `λk1…k5. k4 k1 k2 k3 k5 w`, with w the captured value |
| `ρ` itself (`R ⋈ rho`) | `λk1…k5. k5 k1 k2 k3 k4` |

The chosen arm is handed the four arms not chosen, then the parts. Nothing is dropped behind the program's back, so a linear program (one that uses every variable) can use them. An opened function applied to a value hands back its body as code, with the value spliced in where the variable was. That opening is one rewrite that joins two wires; the body is not walked. Data is written as functions. The net has no constructor agent and no case agent.

**One bare read is 49 rewrites.** The test program reads the identity function, keeps the opened function, and passes four other arms that are a single `ρ` each.

| part of one bare read | rewrites |
|---|---|
| thawing the call (6 applications, `ρ`, 6 arguments) | 13 |
| `ρ` applied, then `R` meets the function; the view is built here | 2 |
| running the view's five binders (5 calls, 15 thaws) | 20 |
| running the chosen arm's five binders (5 calls, 5 thaws) | 10 |
| erasing the four arms not chosen | 4 |

`R ⋈ lam` makes 17 agents (5 `lam`, 5 `app`, 6 `var`, 1 `Lam`) whatever the size of the function read. The other views make 18 (application), 16 (variable) and 14 (`ρ`). **The view costs 21 rewrites: 1 to build and 20 to run. A case agent that held the five arms would do the same in 1.** Erasing the arms not chosen is paid per agent. With four unused arms that each hold 1, 4 or 16 identity functions, the same read takes 97, 145 or 337 rewrites.

**In a reading program the view is 1% of the cost.** `size` counts the binders in any function's code. It recurses through a call-by-value fixpoint (the usual way to get recursion in a λ-calculus) and reads with `ρ` at every node.

| program | rewrites | reads | copies | erases | thaws and calls |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `size suc` | 20,803 | 10 | 8,647 (41.6%) | 11,498 (55.3%) | 635 |
| `size (add 2 2)` | 22,929 | 11 | 9,501 | 12,681 | 723 |
| `size size` | 165,713 | 80 (37 functions, 21 applications, 20 variables, 2 `ρ`) | 69,388 (41.9%) | 91,009 (54.9%) | 5,119 |

Each read costs about 2,070 rewrites: about 865 copies, 1,140 erases, and 64 thaws and calls. **The view is 21 of those, 1.0%. The other 99% is the reading program handling its own code, which never changes.** Each recursive call copies the whole recursive function, with everything it captured. Each read erases the four arms not chosen, agent by agent. Copies and erases together are 97% of `size size`. With four arms instead of five, the same `size size` took 83,020 rewrites, half as many. A self-interpreter written in the calculus costs about 80× for each level (11,502 rewrites for a program of one function call, 942,777 with the interpreter interpreting itself), "nearly all of it copying and erasing the interpreter's own code".

These counts settle three things. No whole-term quote happens: the code is already there, and a read looks at one node at a cost that does not depend on the size of the function. The big λ in the player is the view, a five-way choice written as a five-argument function and built fresh at every read. And the view is not where the cost is. One limit of the counts: the per-read figure comes from one reading program, `size`, on three inputs.

### The view is one layer of Mogensen's quote, made on demand

Quoting exists because the only way to look at a term ["from within the lambda calculus is to apply it to one or more arguments"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=2). So the classical route quotes from outside and reads inside. A quote works by stopping evaluation: ["quotation may add tags that block evaluation, which are then removed by unquote"](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/icfp11.pdf#page=2). Mogensen's 1992 quote writes each node as a function that takes one arm per kind of node and calls the arm for its own kind. In his notation (copied by hand from [p. 348](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=4)), the function λx.M is quoted as `λabc. c (λx. ⌜M⌝)` and the application M N as `λabc. b ⌜M⌝ ⌜N⌝`. The binder stays a real function: ["binding of variables is handled meta-circularly by binding of variables"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=3). His self-interpreter (an interpreter for the calculus, written in the calculus) and self-reducer ran ["about 35 and 50 times slower than direct execution"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=1) under call by need (an argument is run at most once, when first used).

*Inference:* set beside this, the net's view of a function, `λk1…k5. k1 k2 k3 k4 k5 F`, is one layer of the same thing. Both pick the arm for "function" and hand it something that, applied to a value, gives the body with the value where the variable was. There are four differences. Mogensen builds every layer up front, outside the calculus; the net builds one layer when `R` meets the node, and the parts it hands over are raw code. Mogensen has three arms and the net five, because the net reads values of its own running calculus, and opened functions and `ρ` are two more kinds of value. Mogensen's node drops the unused arms for free; the net hands them to the chosen arm, and a program that drops them pays per agent. Mogensen wraps every variable use at quote time; the net's variable arm fires only for a use that holds a captured value.

*Inference:* so "one layer of a Mogensen–Scott encoding, made on demand" names the view's shape correctly, and `ρ` itself is not a quote. A quote takes a term that may still run and makes data of it; `ρ` reads a value whose body never ran. The freezing is done earlier, by the rule that bodies are inert, which is also Jay's fix: identify programs with closed normal forms (closed: no free variables) ["whose reduction begins only when they are applied to arguments"](https://coalg.org/mfps-calco2017/mfps-papers/4-jay.pdf#page=2). The net does have a quote in the plain sense, and it is not `ρ`: by the design notes, a written-out opened function applied to anything hands back its body as code. In the literature's words `ρ` is an intensional operator, one that looks at how a value is built and not only at what it does.

The name "Mogensen–Scott" is later than Mogensen, who calls the constructor coding ["so well-known that the author doesn't know where it originated"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=3). It appears in print as ["a typed Mogensen-Scott encoding"](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/popl17-full.pdf#page=3), with [Wikipedia as the reference](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/popl17-full.pdf#page=14). "Scott" is for Scott's numerals, where a number is its own case function. In the typed account the two directions of one layer are called `in` and `out`, ["the two halves of the isomorphism"](https://lucacardelli.name/Papers/Notes/scott2.pdf#page=1). Wadler's "views" are the source of the word: ["A view allows any type to be viewed as a free data type"](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/views/wadler.pdf#page=1). *Inference:* the net has only the `out` direction, computed on demand. It has no general `in` that builds a function node from parts.

The nearest prior design is Jay's factorisation operator `F`. Where the usual route ["begins by applying a meta-function quote which converts an arbitrary λ-term into a data structure"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=3), SF-calculus (Jay's combinator calculus with the operators S and F) queries a closed normal form ["by using its operator F to reveal its internal structure"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=3). One step looks at one node: an operator selects the first arm, and a compound has its two parts handed to the second. Jay and Palsberg compare it to Lisp: ["factorisation is a formalisation of the car and cdr of Lisp"](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/icfp11.pdf#page=11). *Inference:* `F` and `ρ` both read an unquoted, inert program one node per step. `F` takes its arms as arguments and fires in one step. `ρ` returns a five-argument function that then has to be run, which is the 20 extra rewrites measured.

No paper was found that quotes a λ-term lazily or names a "lazy quote" (two web searches plus the papers read; not exhaustive). Among typed self-interpreters, the one that can take a single evaluation step is the one with a one-layer case: Brown and Palsberg's self-evaluator uses ["a typed Mogensen-Scott encoding, though there are several important differences"](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/popl17-full.pdf#page=8), where their earlier design used [a fold-style encoding](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/popl16-full.pdf#page=7) that walks a whole term. None of the typed papers reports a running time. Mogensen's 35 and 50 are the only measured self-interpreter slowdowns in the notes.

A function can also be read with no source kept: apply it to a fresh variable and rebuild a term from what comes out. Berger and Schwichtenberg's procedure does this by ["inverting the evaluation functional and returning normal forms only"](https://www.math.lmu.de/~schwicht/papers/lics91/paper.pdf#page=1). *Inference:* this and the net's read start with the same move. In the net the body is inert, so the application hands back the body's source. In normalization by evaluation the body runs on the marker, so only its behaviour comes back. The net can get the second from the first by interpreting the opened body. The reverse is not possible.

### Trees read the compiled combinator; the compile step is where the quote went

Jay's claim is that tree calculus gets self-evaluation ["without any of the usual outside machinery, such as the Gödel numbering of Turing machines, the quotation of lambda-abstractions or serialisation of programs"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=5). The claim covers normal forms. Such calculi ["represent all their Turing-computable functions whose domain is limited to normal forms"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/14486/1/2010003973.pdf#page=1), and a term that never settles ["does not reduce to a factorable form"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/14486/1/2010003973.pdf#page=10) (factorable: a shape the read rule accepts). An outside reader says the same: ["intensional operations act only on normal forms"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.09302#page=199). Adding the operator to λ-calculus without that limit ["breaks confluence"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03861#page=6) (confluence: every order of rewriting ends in the same result). For terms that can still run, Jay's own lazy evaluator falls back on a quote: they ["will be quoted"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=76), and ["quotation of computations is not definable within tree calculus"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=76).

One rewrite reads one node. Tree calculus has three rules, and the third ["decomposes the first argument to expose its branches"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=40). No view is built, because the choice is the rule. *Inference:* on this one point trees do better than the net, and the reason is "the case is a primitive", not "the program is a tree". The net's own counts already say a case agent would do the same in one rewrite.

What a tree program sees when it reads a function written with λ is the output of bracket abstraction. In Jay's book ["the bracket abstraction \[x\]t represents the function that, when applied to x yields t"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=53), and λ in the SF papers [is notation for its shortcut form, star abstraction](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/14486/1/2010003973.pdf#page=6). It is not part of the calculus: it ["is not a “first-class” means of constructing terms"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=54) and ["does not preserve the meaning of terms"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=54). Keeping the binder visible takes extra tags: the conversion ["can be made intensional, too, by adding tags to record the presence of abstractions and combinators"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=13). *Inference:* in Jay's calculi the work a quote would do is split in two. Bracket abstraction, at compile time and outside the calculus, removes the binders and makes the program a normal form. The read rule, at run time and inside the calculus, then takes that normal form apart. "No quotation" speaks of the second half. No source calls bracket abstraction a quote; that link is drawn in the research notes.

The compile step is costly in size. Jay's figure for the plain algorithm in tree calculus is size 5k − 2 for a combinator of size k, and ["This five-fold expansion is especially problematic when abstractions are nested"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=54). For any fixed, finite set of combinators, n log n ["is a lower bound on the translation size"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.03794#page=3). The plain S and K algorithm is [cubic in the worst case](https://ejournals.eu/en/journal/reports-on-mathematical-logic/article/on-the-complexity-of-the-standard-translation-of-lambda-calculus-into-combinatory-logic#panel-abstract-en) and Turner's is [quadratic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.03794#page=5). Linear size is claimed only with an unbounded family of ["bulk combinators"](https://okmij.org/ftp/tagless-final/ski.pdf#page=14) and, by Lester's account, a size measure that ["inflates the size of the lambda term"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.03217#page=7). Measured in this project (`DESIGN.md`): the term λx1…xn. k xn…x1 at n = 16 is 50 agents as a net, 1,134 nodes in the project's 9-rule tree calculus and 6,533 in tree calculus. *Inference:* this is a routing cost. A tree has no pointer from a binder to its uses, so every application on the way carries a routing node. The net pays none of it because a variable is a wire.

Trees do not add computing power either. Rowe gave ["a faithful encoding of the Factorisation Calculus into the"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.06713#page=1) λ-calculus, at the price of ["a large increase in the size of terms"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.06713#page=14). He also grants Jay's point that `F` cannot be defined away as a macro: ["we ask is F eliminable? The answer to this question is no"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.06713#page=13). *Inference:* trees add nothing over "λ plus a way to read code". They change where the encoding sits (in the compile step and in the rules) and what is lost (the binder). Church's δ, `F`, the tree rules and the net's `ρ` are the same kind of move: add one primitive that inspects a stable term.

Trees also do not remove the cost the net's counts point at. Jay writes that tree rules ["commonly take one step to copy, delete or permute combinations", so that "a single computation step may take an arbitrarily long time"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=138), and that the obvious fix is sharing by pointers, [left for later](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=139). *Inference:* tree calculus counts a whole copy as one step, so its step counts hide what the net counts agent by agent. The design notes say where the trees' real saving comes from: "Trees get this for free from hash-consing; a net copies for real" (hash-consing: store each distinct piece once and find it by its hash). That saving is sharing. The same notes say the net's inert code could be shared the same way, and that "It isn't built." No published speed figure for a self-interpreter in SF-calculus or tree calculus was found, only sizes: [1,185 operators](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/icfp11.pdf#page=9) for the typed one that runs a quoted program, and [508 nodes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/barry-jay-personal/tree-calculus/master/tree_book.pdf#page=76) for the tree-calculus `size`.

### Only λSF abstracts lazily; the net opens binders

Lazy bracket abstraction as a reader exists once in the sources. The λSF paper sets out to factorise functions ["by converting them to combinators when it is safe to do so"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=3). Read with `F`, a function gives a fixed marker as its left part and [the star abstraction of its body](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=6) as its right part. Before any read the function is left alone: λx.λy.y ["is already a closed normal form"](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/122938/4/1-s2.0-S1571066116300913-main%20%281%29.pdf#page=5). Searches for bracket abstraction done at run time, on demand or lazily found nothing else.

*Inference:* the net's reader is not this. A λSF read rewrites the top node of the body into variable-free form: it pushes the λ one level down or turns it into a combinator. The binder is consumed. A read in the net relabels the function as opened and leaves the body alone. Applying the opened function to a marker returns the body with the marker where the variable was, and reading the opened function closes it again.

The standard name for that is opening. In the locally nameless style (bound variables are numbers, free variables are names), ["Variable opening turns some bound variables into free variables"](https://www.chargueraud.org/research/2009/ln/main.pdf#page=5) and ["is used to investigate the body of an abstraction"](https://www.chargueraud.org/research/2009/ln/main.pdf#page=5); the reader supplies the marker. McBride and McKinna call the pair `instantiate` and `abstract`, which ["enable us to navigate under binders and back out again"](https://www.e-pig.org/downloads/notanum.pdf#page=5). When binders are functions of the host language (higher-order abstract syntax), opening is plain application, and substitution is [inherited from the host](http://web.cs.ucla.edu/~palsberg/paper/popl16-full.pdf#page=7). On first-order terms each opening walks the body: ["The implementation of variable opening needs to traverse a term recursively"](https://www.chargueraud.org/research/2009/ln/main.pdf#page=5), and closing ["follows a pattern similar to"](https://www.chargueraud.org/research/2009/ln/main.pdf#page=6) it. Measured: in the net opening is one rewrite that joins two wires. *Inference:* that is because the binder node is already wired to the variable's uses. The net's "close" is not the standard closing. It only puts an unapplied opened function back under its binder. Nothing re-binds a marker once it is spliced into code, which is the higher-order-syntax position, where ["you cannot inspect the bindings of variables"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4727ED102FD8F5C5950EBBFE776E8C69/S0956796800000423a.pdf/efficient_selfinterpretation_in_lambda_calculus.pdf#page=5).

*Inference:* bracket abstraction and quoting are related as follows. Both turn a λ-term, which can still run under its binder, into a stable form another program can take apart. A quote gives data that has to be unquoted or interpreted to run; bracket abstraction gives a term that still runs. A quote keeps the source exactly; bracket abstraction keeps the behaviour and drops the binder. Both are done outside the calculus, and λSF is the one case where the step is a rule.

### Inside interaction nets, no published reader hands back code as data

In an interaction net each agent has one principal port, and a rewrite happens only where two agents meet on their principal ports. Lafont's interaction combinators are the three-agent system (constructor, duplicator, eraser) that every other such system [can be translated into](https://dcreager.net/pdf/Lafont1997.pdf#page=14). Of the systems named below, HVM1 and HVM2 are interaction-net evaluators, Inpla is an interaction-net interpreter, and Ivy is the net language under the Vine language.

| mechanism | what reads what | cost of one read | what it can and cannot see | where it is done |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The net's thaw `T` is one (inference) |

No way to look at it without expanding it (inference) | HVM2's REF; [Ivy's global agents](https://vine.dev/docs/ivy-ivm/interaction-system#expand) |

The search result is a negative. Eleven searches found no paper or system in which a net inspects another net's code and gets the answer back as data it can branch on. That is a search result, not a proof of absence. *Inference:* the net's `ρ` has no published counterpart inside interaction nets. Lafont's decoder opens a code but returns the net itself, never a description of it. Salikhmetov's reader walks a net but leaves text that no rule can take apart.

*Inference:* three parts of the net do have names in this literature. Thaw is an evaluation token. In token-passing nets ["an evaluation token that traverses the syntactical representation of the term"](https://webarchive.di.uminho.pt/haslab.uminho.pt/jba/files/07tnfp.pdf#page=3) turns each inert agent into its running twin, and under weak evaluation it [does not go under a binder](https://webarchive.di.uminho.pt/haslab.uminho.pt/jba/files/07tnfp.pdf#page=9). This is also why the net needs no box around a body: inert agents cannot react until a token reaches them, the point Sinot makes for his encoding (["no artifact to represent boxes is needed"](https://web.archive.org/web/20240416005138/https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1571066106000934#aep-abstract-sec-id6)). The reader is not a token. It fires once at the top agent and is consumed, so it is an ordinary destructor with five rules. And the view is Lafont's menu and selector written with λ agents: the program supplies five arms, one is selected, the rest are erased. Lafont's version pays the same erasing cost, cell by cell.

*Inference:* a read has to be a family of rules. In a pure interaction system the only thing one agent learns about another is which rule fired when they met. There is no "what are you?" besides interaction, so a reflection primitive needs one rule per kind of agent read, which is how `R` is built. The theory of observation has the same limit: an observer is ["simply another net C whose interface is big enough"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.0380#page=2), and what it can tell apart is behaviour, never syntax. The net's `ρ` tells apart two functions with the same behaviour and different code. That is a deliberate break with this theory.

Copying agent by agent is the declared design of interaction nets, not an accident: ["weakening should gradually erase a proof and contraction should gradually copy a proof"](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3f5ad5e-9466-4ddf-8869-14d34ea18782/content#page=2), and ["external machinery to copy or erase nets is absent from the system"](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3f5ad5e-9466-4ddf-8869-14d34ea18782/content#page=2). Boxes and closed reduction decide when a body may be copied; the copy itself is still per agent. Lafont's code is a second, inert net that ["can be freely duplicated"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.0380#page=16) and must be decoded before it runs. *Inference:* neither makes copying cheaper than one rewrite per agent. The one published exception is the reference agent of HVM2 and Ivy, taken up in the next section.

### From outside the net: walk it, match it, nest it, or mirror it

| mechanism | what reads what | cost of one read | what it can and cannot see | inside an interaction net? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| read-back from outside | the implementer [walks the final graph and rebuilds the term](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.04240#page=6) | believed linear in the size of the term read, ["but there is no proof of this fact"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.04240#page=6) | everything, but the running program cannot use it (inference) | no; the context the reader carries is ["not a part of the algorithm"](https://web.archive.org/web/20240822152938id_/https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/96709.96711#page=5) |
| token machine (geometry of interaction) | a token walks a graph that [stays fixed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.00427#page=2) | no rewrites and [no garbage collection](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/16874549/Reversible_irreversible_and_optimal_machines.pdf#page=4), but it [can be exponentially slower](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12988#page=2) | computes the answer; never copies or changes the graph | no: no source runs the token as agents inside a net |
| observation by a context | a net is plugged into [another net](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.0380#page=2) | not a read | behaviour only, never syntax (inference from [what Mazza takes as observable](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.0380#page=3)) | this is the theory of what nets can tell apart |
| rules that are graphs (port graphs) | a rule matches a sub-graph, and [rules are graphs that can be rewritten too](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00418560#authors-detailed) | sub-graph matching, not constant time (inference) | whatever the pattern names (inference) | no |
| frame variables (hierarchical graphs) | a rule variable stands for a whole nested graph | 1 rule application (inference) | copies or removes it ["without looking at"](https://user.informatik.uni-bremen.de/hof/papers/JCSS02.pdf#page=2) the contents | no. "Binets" nest nets inside agents, and then [a rule can touch everything nested](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.6339#page=6) |
| nets as tokens (Valk) | an outer Petri net (a net of places and tokens) holds inner nets as its tokens | 1 shared event | only the events the inner net offers: ["both the system and the object participate in the same event"](https://www2.informatik.uni-hamburg.de/tgi/publikationen/public/data/1998/Valk98/Valk98.pdf#page=12) | no |
| reflective Petri nets | a fixed meta-level net reads a copy of the base net kept as tokens | the copy [is updated on every base step](https://cazzola.di.unimi.it/pubs/jucs07-www.pdf#page=7) | the whole structure and state, to [observe and change](https://cazzola.di.unimi.it/pubs/jucs07-www.pdf#page=7) it | no |

*Inference:* three of these bear on the net. A token machine is the extreme answer to the copying cost: never copy code, keep one fixed graph, and move a token that remembers where it is. The sources' verdict is that this trades copying for repeated walking. The published middle ground is a machine in which ["the token holds control over graph rewriting"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.00427#page=3) and rewrites only the parts it visits. Frame variables are the graph-rewriting form of a reference: one step copies or deletes a nested graph without looking inside. And reflective Petri nets keep a second copy of the system as data and pay to keep it in step. The net has one copy of the code and reads it directly. Systems where rules are graphs can read rules because their one primitive, sub-graph matching, already reads arbitrary structure. Interaction nets have no such primitive.

## What each mechanism would change here, largest measured cost first

None of the following was built or measured. The order follows the counts for the one reading program measured: copies and erases are 97%, the view is 1%, and the last items are costs that rewrite counts do not show.

### Code held by reference is aimed at the 97%

The standard implementation techniques all do one thing: keep unchanging code in one place, and let a function value be a small reference to it plus the values it captured. Landin's closure is the λ-expression together with ["the environment relative to which it was evaluated"](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~crary/819-f09/Landin64.pdf#page=10). Closure conversion makes a closure ["a data structure containing pure code and a representation of its environment"](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rwh/papers/closures/popl96.pdf#page=1). A supercombinator (a function with no free variables) gets ["a fixed code sequence"](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1987/01/slpj-book-1987-small.pdf#page=236). Hash-consing ["is a technique to share values that are structurally equal"](https://usr.lmf.cnrs.fr/~jcf/ftp/publis/hash-consing2.pdf#page=1). Unison (a language that stores code by its hash) identifies each definition ["by a hash of its syntax tree"](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea/#title).

An interaction-net evaluator already does this. HVM2's reference agents ["represent an immutable net that is expanded in a single interaction"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=5), added because copying with copier agents ["brings considerable overhead"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=7). A reference is copied or erased as one node. The limit: a reference whose net contains copier agents is not copied as a pointer; ["it instead is computed as a normal call operation"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=7). Ivy's global agents are the same idea, with the condition that the copier's label ["does not appear in the global net"](https://vine.dev/docs/ivy-ivm/interaction-system). In both systems the definitions are closed code, fixed before the run. No system was found that shares code built at run time by hashing.

The first column below is from the sources. The other two are inference from what each value is made of.

| technique | a function value is made of | copying or dropping it costs (inference) | what a program could read from it (inference) |
|---|---|---|---|
| the net today | its whole code as agents, with captured values written into the variable nodes | every agent of the code, each time (measured) | every node, one per `ρ` |
| [closure](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rwh/papers/closures/popl96.pdf#page=1) | a pointer to shared closed code, plus an environment | the environment only | nothing in most languages; code and environment if a reader is added |
| [defunctionalization](https://www.brics.dk/RS/01/23/BRICS-RS-01-23.pdf#page=9) (each function becomes a tag, each call a case on the tag) | a tag naming the λ, plus the captured values | the tag and the captured values | which λ it is, the captured values, and the body through a table |
| [lambda lifting](https://www.brics.dk/RS/03/26/BRICS-RS-03-26.pdf#page=3) (free variables become extra parameters), supercombinators | the name of a closed top-level function, plus its arguments so far | the name and the arguments | the body, through the table of definitions |
| [hash-consing](https://usr.lmf.cnrs.fr/~jcf/ftp/publis/hash-consing2.pdf#page=1) | a pointer to the one shared copy of each distinct node | one pointer; freeing is the collector's job | every node as before, plus equality of two pieces of code in one step |
| [content addressing](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/language-reference/hashes/#title) (Unison) | a hash naming a stored, unchanging definition | one hash | the stored tree, looked up by hash |
| [reference agent](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=5) (HVM2, Ivy) | one agent naming a closed net in a static table | one rewrite | not readable in HVM2; a reader could index the table |

*Inference:* the options for the net follow, smallest change first. (1) Reference agents for closed code. A closed function, such as the recursive `size` once its free variables are made parameters, becomes one agent. Copying it and erasing it are one rewrite each. Thaw would expand it only when it is called, and a read would look into the shared definition. This has the HVM2 and Ivy precedent. The notes argue that HVM2's no-copier condition holds here because bodies never rewrite before the call. That was not tested, and bodies do hold `sig` agents (the split of a variable used twice), which start copying when a value arrives.

*Inference:* (2) Code kept apart from captured values. The net writes captured values into the body, so a function that captured something is no longer the same code as its source, and a reference can stand only for the closed part. With the body shared and the captured values in a small separate structure, a copy would cost the number of captured values. Opening a function would then return shared code plus a pending substitution, the form Mœbius uses for open code (["closures consisting of a variable and a delayed substitution"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.08099#page=2)), instead of a spliced copy.

*Inference:* (3) Hash-consing of inert code, which the design notes already propose. It is the run-time form of (1): it also shares code built during the run, and it would give reading programs a one-step test for equal code. It needs a nameless standard form for binders, because in the net variables are wires, and a way to free unused entries ([weak pointers](https://usr.lmf.cnrs.fr/~jcf/ftp/publis/hash-consing2.pdf#page=3) in the library read). (4) Content addressing is (3) with the table kept across runs. It adds nothing to the rewrite count of one run.

*Inference:* sharing would not change what programs can observe. Bodies never change, and code identity is already visible through `ρ`, so storing identical code once is invisible to a program. Several mechanisms from the map would not reduce the 97%. Boxes and closed reduction only decide when a body may be copied. Lafont's codes are copied agent by agent like anything else. A move to trees helps only through the sharing that comes with a hash-consed tree store. A token machine removes copying by never rewriting, at the risk of the exponential slowdown noted above.

Erases are the larger half of the count (about 1,140 of 2,070 per read), and the bare-read figures show them growing with the size of the arms (97, 145, 337). *Inference:* arms held by reference would cost four erases a read, whatever they hold. A native case does not remove this cost by itself. HVM2's switch and Ivy's branch both [hand the arm not taken to an eraser](https://vine.dev/docs/ivy-ivm/interaction-system#call), and HVM2 relies on references to make that cheap: ["Since REFs unfold lazily, the program above will properly erase the foo_S branch"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=23). The counts do not say how many of the erases are unused arms and how many are other dropped code; that split was not measured. The design notes add that a program written linearly "pays neither" cost, and that such programs are written by hand, with no compiler from ordinary programs. This research did not look at that route.

### A case agent would turn the view's 21 rewrites into 1, which is 1% today

Measured: the view takes 1 rewrite to build and 20 to run, and a case agent that held the five arms would do the same in 1. *Inference:* that is Jay's `F` design, and it is the standard form in interaction nets. The five `R` rules already are a case on the code agents. What is unusual is their right-hand side, which builds a function-encoded value where a case agent would continue into the chosen arm. HVM2 made numbers native on similar grounds: emulating numeric operations with function encodings ["would be very inefficient"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HigherOrderCO/HVM/main/paper/HVM2.pdf#page=5). Nested patterns would save writing, not rewrites, since they compile to one rewrite per level. Wildcard rules cannot replace the five rules, since they cannot report which agent was met. By this report's arithmetic, if copies and erases fell to near zero, the view would be about 21 of the roughly 66 rewrites left per read. So the case agent matters after the copying is dealt with, not before.

### The 80× per interpreter level is the same copying, and thaw is already the way back down

Measured: a self-interpreter in the net costs about 80× per level. Mogensen measured 35×. *Inference:* the gap is the copying. His reducer was call by need, which shares the interpreter's code where the net copies it, and the view is 1% of the net's cost. Both are constant factors, which is what [Mogensen later showed](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/linear-time-self-interpretation-of-the-pure-lambda-calculus-lectu/#main-content) can be achieved (verified from the abstract only).

Systems with levels say the same about naive towers. In Maude ["simulating a single step of rewriting at one level involves many rewriting steps one level up"](https://maude.lcc.uma.es/maude-manual/maude-manualch17.html#x114-234002), so it ships built-in descent functions that ["provide useful and efficient ways of reducing metalevel computations to object-level ones"](https://maude.lcc.uma.es/maude-manual/maude-manualch17.html#x114-241003). Smith's 3-Lisp tower of interpreters ["can be finitely implemented"](https://ics.uci.edu/~jajones/INF102-S18/readings/17_Smith84.pdf#page=1), and staging can ["collapse this tower into a compiler that removes all interpretive overhead"](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/rompf/papers/amin-popl18.pdf#page=1). *Inference:* thaw is the net's descent function, since it runs code natively. A program pays the tower cost only if it re-implements evaluation with `ρ` where it could hand code to thaw. The tower papers remove the cost of interpretive dispatch. The net's measured cost is mostly copying, which the sharing options above address.

### Reading everything has a price in equations, and sealing is the known way to limit it

This is not a rewrite cost. It is the main design cost the sources describe. Wand proved that in a λ-calculus with a facility that can read any term, [two terms are interchangeable only when they are the same text up to renaming](https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/home/wand/pubs.html#Wand98). Kavvos restates it as ["Theorem 2 (Wand)"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.09302#page=20) and notes that the quoting function in the proof ["is a Scott-Mogensen encoding"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.09302#page=20). Shutt's general form: for the set S of objects that can be taken apart, ["equivalence of objects in S is trivial"](https://dcreager.net/pdf/Shutt2010.pdf#page=354). Taha shows the clash from the optimizer's side: a test for "is this code an application" is ["in direct contention with allowing the"](https://web.archive.org/web/20161018191554id_/https://www.cs.rice.edu/~taha/publications/thesis/thesis.pdf#page=113) β-rule (the rule that runs a function call) inside code.

*Inference:* the net loses less than Wand's language. `ρ` reads values only. Its argument is run first, so a running application cannot be read, and equations about running computations survive. The theory is trivial on function bodies: `ρ` tells a function whose body is `I I` from one whose body is `I` (`I` is the identity function), though the two bodies rewrite to the same thing. The evaluator loses nothing, since it never rewrote under a binder. What is lost is for a compiler: any rewrite of a function body can be seen by a program. And the net has no information hiding, since a caller that holds `ρ` can read any callee.

The known ways to limit this both seal something. Kernel seals some objects: fexprs there ["are unable to deconstruct either encapsulated objects"](https://dcreager.net/pdf/Shutt2010.pdf#page=355) (environments, closures, running computations). The modal designs mark what is readable: □A ["represents code of type A"](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~fp/papers/jacm00.pdf#page=3) while ordinary functions stay opaque, and in Mœbius ["meta-programs can pattern match on code"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.08099#page=3). *Inference:* in those terms every λ in the net is a box, thaw is "unbox and run", and `ρ` is a one-level match on code. A sealed kind of value, or a limit on who may hold `ρ`, would be the net's version of either design. Neither was looked at further.

### Some mechanisms would change what is read, not what a read costs

*Inference:* trees or run-time bracket abstraction would make the reader see routing combinators where it now sees the binder, at a size cost the project has measured. Normalization by evaluation would show behaviour only. A real closing step, one that re-binds a marker inside arbitrary code as the locally nameless `abstract` does, is something the net lacks; whether it could have one was not looked at. Reflective towers hand over more than the net does: the call's text, the environment and the continuation (the rest of the computation), where `ρ` hands over one node of a function's code. A mirrored copy of the program kept as data (reflective Petri nets, Maude's `upTerm`) is what the net avoids by reading the only copy.

## What is not known or not verified

**About this net.** Nothing was built. No source measures hash-consing or reference agents inside a reflective interaction-net reducer, so every saving named above is inferred from the net's own counts. Whether shared code clashes with linear use of code (a linear program expects to own what it holds) was not found in any source. Whether HVM2's no-copier condition holds for every function body is an untested inference. The per-read figure comes from one reading program on three inputs, and the erases were not split by cause. No source works out which equations survive when reflection is limited to values under weak call-by-value evaluation, with captured values inside the code; Shutt's chapter 15 is the closest. No source measures opening and closing on a graph where variables are wires.

**Negative search results.** "No published reflection inside interaction nets", "no lazy quote", "no run-time bracket abstraction besides λSF" and "nobody calls bracket abstraction a quote" are search results, not proofs of absence. A likely second case of run-time routing, Fernández, Mackie and Sinot's λ-calculus with director strings (2005), could not be fetched and is not verified. No source was found that says who first wrote the name "Mogensen–Scott". Reflection in term-rewriting systems other than Maude was not searched.

**Sources read only in part or second hand.** Wand's 1998 paper exists as PostScript only, so its body was not machine-checked. Only the abstract on his page and the restatements by Kavvos and Shutt are verified. Two things from that body are therefore not verified: that his fexpr hands back its operand in "Mogensen-Scott coding" (which would make it the closest prior primitive to `ρ`, and a 1998 use of the name), and his remark that an implementation may still optimize as long as the original source can be recovered. Mogensen's 2000 paper and Jay's 2019 survey are verified from abstracts only, and the constant factor in the first is unknown. The seconds in Mogensen's 1992 table did not survive text extraction; only the ratios 1, 35 and 50 are safe. Mogensen's schema and Jay's `F` rules were copied by hand and checked through the prose next to them. Lafont's figures, which show how menus, selectors, codes and the decoder are wired, are not in the extracted text, and his paper and Wadler's were checked against copies on third-party sites. The bodies of Sinot's token-passing papers and of Mackie's 2004 and 2008 evaluators were not read, only abstracts. The lower bounds for bracket abstraction (Joy, Rayward-Smith and Burton; Kennaway and Sleep; Noshita; Hikita) are given as Czajka and Kiselyov report them, and whether the n log n bound carries over to tree calculus's single node was not checked. Meredith and Radestock's 2005 paper, Clavel and Meseguer's 2002 paper, Johnsson 1985, Hughes 1982, Kleene 1936 and Mitchell's full paper were not fetched. Each is cited through a later or partial source.

**Numbers the sources do not have.** No running-time figure was found for a self-interpreter in SF-calculus or tree calculus, in the three typed self-interpreter papers, for reading code in the staged and modal papers, for 3-Lisp towers, or for Maude's meta-level. No published split of a token-passing run into token moves against copies and erases exists to set beside the net's 97%. No count was found of the share of HVM2's or Ivy's rewrites that are copies and erases, with or without references. The cost of read-back is stated as believed linear, without proof.

**Systems not checked.** How Bend and Vine (languages on top of HVM2 and Ivy) compile data types and `match`, and whether Bend lifts every closed function body into its own definition, could not be verified; the old Bend documentation is gone from its address. The variant of tree calculus in which triage (the three-way test for leaf, stem or fork) is a rewrite rule, Jay's 2025 paper on typed program analysis, and a `size size = 168` figure seen in a search summary were not verified. Mackie's geometry-of-interaction machine (1995), Gonthier, Abadi and Lévy (1992) and the Asperti–Guerrini book could not be quoted. HVM3, HVM4, BOHM, inets and PIN were not fetched. The Maude manual was fetched with certificate checking off.
