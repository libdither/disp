The adds, compared

Every add written for the nets so far, on two numbers of 2 to 1,024 bits. Each is named for the kind of program it is, the shape of its numbers and the way it adds. A linear program uses every variable, so it erases nothing. An ordinary one tests bits and drops what it does not pick. A reversible one is linear and hands the first number back beside the sum, so a subtract can undo it. The numbers are lists of bits or trees of bits, and a tree is added one bit after another or in parallel.

In the charts, colour is the shape of the numbers and the way they are added. A solid line is a linear program, a dashed one an ordinary program, and the dotted one the linear list add whose two numbers have different shapes. The reversible adds and subtracts are left out of the charts: each runs a little above the linear add it is built on. They are in the tables.

A round fires every pair that is waiting when it starts. With no limit on how many that is, the rounds are the least time a run can take, and the work is what it costs whatever the order. An add that goes one bit after another takes rounds in step with the bits. The parallel adds take a fixed number of rounds more each time the numbers double, and pay for it in work and in room.
