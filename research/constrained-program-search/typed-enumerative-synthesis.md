# Type-directed and enumerative program synthesis with learned guidance (classical PL through neural-guided enumeration, as of October 2026)

Scope note for the report writer. This file covers how synthesis systems structure search over programs, where typing information enters (pruning, ranking, decomposition), how learned guidance is combined with hard constraints, and what the complexity theory says about the type-inhabitation oracle. Universal-search theory (Solomonoff, Levin, Hutter, OOPS, PowerPlay) is deliberately excluded; the project covers it elsewhere. Every link below was fetch-verified with `scripts/verify-source.sh -q` and points at the minted page/anchor fragment; the per-source ledger (quote, status, relevance) is at the end, together with the list of sources that could NOT be verified and must not be linked. Years are given per result; the field's "state as of October 2026" is summarized in the takeaways.

The project framing this serves: a dependently typed language (disp) where a type is an ordinary program that answers yes/no about a value, type checking runs as a stream of cheap steps under a budget, and the optimizer enumerates per-hole candidate streams ordered by a prior, checks them against a goal recognizer, and on failure reifies the conflict (tree position, expected type, actual type) into a witness from which a pruning predicate is learned. Where a finding bears on that design, the "Inferences" subsections say so explicitly and label it as inference.

## Key question 1: Type-directed synthesis (Synquid, Myth, Smyth, λ², Hoogle+, Leon, type inhabitation, Hazel). How do refinement/dependent types prune, and what decomposition rules make it work left to right?

### Takeaway
Every successful type-directed synthesizer splits the term language into two syntactic classes and runs them in opposite directions: introduction forms (constructors, lambdas, matches, conditionals) are *refined* top-down by pushing the goal type (and, since 2015, the examples) into subgoals, while elimination forms (variables, applications) are *guessed* by enumerating well-typed normal forms bottom-up; refinement types then add a per-node satisfiability check (via an SMT or liquid-type solver) that rejects a partial program before its holes are filled. Since 2019 the frontier has been (a) replacing SMT-per-candidate with cheaper abstractions that are refined only when a candidate fails (Hoogle+'s abstract types, Blaze's abstract semantics, ECTA automata), (b) making bottom-up search work for recursive programs (Burst's angelic recursion), and (c) feeding the hole's static context to an LLM (Hazel, 2024).

### Cited Findings

**Myth (Osera & Zdancewic, PLDI 2015)** — type-and-example-directed synthesis.
- The synthesis judgment is split, "as in bidirectional typechecking", into refinement of introduction forms and *guessing* of elimination forms; the text states "'Guessing' in this context amounts to well-typed term enumeration. Because the ill-typed terms vastly outnumber the well-typed terms", generation proceeds "by choosing a particular term shape and then recursively generating its components" — [Myth, p.1](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~stevez/papers/OZ15.pdf#page=1); the rule names are EGUESS APP / EGUESS VAR / IREFINE GUESS (same PDF).
- Guessing "only generates β-normal forms: terms that can be reduced no further", and the paper relates the algorithm "to the analysis of normal form proofs" — [Myth, p.2](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~stevez/papers/OZ15.pdf#page=2).
- Examples are pushed through constructors, `match`, and lambdas as "example worlds"; a *refinement tree* data structure caches the decomposition. Evaluation: "more than 40 benchmarks" — [Myth, p.2](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~stevez/papers/OZ15.pdf#page=2).

**Example-directed synthesis as proof search (Frankle, Osera, Walker, Zdancewic, POPL 2016).**
- "examples can, in general, be interpreted as refinement types", and example-directed synthesis is "proof search in a sequent calculus with intersection and union" types; the paper lists three strategies for that proof search: "bottom-up theorem-proving, term enumeration with refinement type checking, and combinations of both" — [Frankle et al., p.1](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~dpw/papers/type-synthesis-popl-2016.pdf#page=1). The calculus is a focused ("synchronized") sequent calculus proven sound and complete for the synthesis problem (same page).

**Synquid (Polikarpova, Kuraj, Solar-Lezama, PLDI 2016)** — synthesis from polymorphic refinement types.
- Mechanism: the type system combines "bidirectional checking with top-down propagation of overapproximate type information, resulting in a round-trip type checking mechanism, which promotes modular checking of function applications" — [Synquid, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=2).
- Pruning before holes are filled: "due to round-trip type checking (Sec. 3.2), this assignment is discovered before the two arguments to insert are synthesized" — [Synquid, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=5); for higher-order components such as `foldr`, "round-trip type checking handles its higher-order argument, f, in a special way" — [Synquid, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=6).
- Term classes: following Myth-style bidirectional typing, terms are split into E-terms (variables, applications) that "propagate type information bottom-up" and I-terms that propagate top-down; conditionals are synthesized by *liquid abduction* (inferring the branch guard as the weakest refinement that makes the branch type check) — [Synquid PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=2).
- Recursion: the FIX rule "comes with a termination check. In the context of synthesis, termination concerns are impossible to ignore, since non-terminating recursive programs are always simpler than terminating ones"; recursive calls are typed at a "termination-weakened type" S≺ — [Synquid PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=2).
- Evaluation: Synquid "was able to synthesize (and fully verify) solutions for all 64 benchmarks"; "all but 7 benchmarks are synthesized within 5 seconds"; in the ablation "without this feature [MUSFix] 37 out of 64 benchmarks time out" — [Synquid, p.14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=14).

**ReSyn (Knoth, Wang, Polikarpova, Hoffmann, PLDI 2019)** — resource-guided synthesis.
- The type system "combines polymorphic refinement types with potential annotations of automatic amortized resource analysis"; synthesis uses "the user-provided resource bound to guide the search, eagerly rejecting incomplete programs that consume too many resources"; "both synthesis and resource analysis are undecidable in theory and expensive in practice" — [ReSyn, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07415#page=1). "Since potentials are attached to types, type polymorphism gives us resource polymorphism for free" — [ReSyn, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07415#page=5).

**λ² (Feser, Chaudhuri, Dillig, PLDI 2015)** — data-structure transformations from examples.
- "Our approach combines three technical ideas: inductive generalization, deduction, and enumerative search": examples are generalized "into hypotheses" (skeletons with holes) "in a type-aware manner", deduction both *refutes* hypotheses (e.g. `map f* x` cannot send `[1,1]` to `[2,3]`) and *infers new examples* for the holes, and best-first enumeration orders hypotheses; the output "is guaranteed to be the least-cost program in our language to fit the examples" — [λ², p.1](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~swarat/pubs/pldi15.pdf#page=1).

**Leon (Kneuss, Kuraj, Kuncak, Suter, OOPSLA 2013)** — synthesis modulo recursive functions.
- Synthesis from pre/post-condition specifications over unbounded ADTs via "cost-guided application of deductive synthesis rules" and "counterexample-guided strategies", integrated with the Leon verifier that "reports concrete counterexamples" — [Leon, p.2](https://lara.epfl.ch/~kuncak/papers/KneussETAL13SynthesisModuloRecursiveFunctions.pdf#page=2).

**InSynth (Gvero, Kuncak, Kuraj, Piskac, PLDI 2013)** — "Complete Completion using Types and Weights".
- Code completion as type inhabitation ranked by weights: "we introduce weights to guide the search and rank the presented solutions" — [InSynth, p.2](https://lara.epfl.ch/~kuncak/papers/GveroETAL13CompleteCompletionTypesWeights.pdf#page=2); the weight of a term `λx1…xm. f e1…en` is "the sum of the weights of all elements that occur in the expression" — [InSynth, p.5](https://lara.epfl.ch/~kuncak/papers/GveroETAL13CompleteCompletionTypesWeights.pdf#page=5). This is the earliest clean instance of "type inhabitation oracle + additive prior" in the PL literature.

**Smyth (Lubin, Collins, Omar, Chugh, ICFP 2020)** — program sketching with live bidirectional evaluation (Hazel group).
- "live bidirectional evaluation, propagates examples 'backward' through partially evaluated sketches", which lets Smyth "(a) synthesize recursive functions without trace-complete sets of examples and (b) specify and solve interdependent synthesis goals"; on Myth's benchmarks "Smyth requires on average 66% of the number of expert examples required by Myth", and "Leon and Synqid, two state-of-the-art logic-based synthesis tools, fail to complete several tasks on which Smyth succeeds" — [Smyth, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.00583#page=1). Smyth generalizes "the Myth hole synthesis" procedure — [Smyth, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.00583#page=4).

**Hazel typed holes (Omar, Voysey, Chugh, Hammer, POPL 2019; Blinn, Li, Kim, Omar, OOPSLA 2024).**
- Hazel gives "a static semantics for incomplete programs" and evaluates "an incomplete functional program past the first hole" — [Live Functional Programming with Typed Holes, p.3–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00155#page=3).
- 2024: Hazel is "a typed functional programming environment designed specifically around typed holes, inserting them automatically to ensure total syntax error recovery [47] and total type error recovery"; the LLM is given the *typing context of the hole* (expected type plus transitively relevant type definitions retrieved via the language server), evaluated on MVUBench — [Blinn et al., p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00921#page=4); without such context the model "will hallucinate plausible-but-incorrect definitions" — [Blinn et al., p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00921#page=3).

**Hoogle+ / TYGAR (Guo et al., POPL 2020)** — type-guided abstraction refinement.
- "we introduce type-guided abstraction refinement (TYGAR), a new approach to scalable type-directed synthesis": build a graph over *abstract* types (each standing for an unbounded set of concrete types), search by reachability, and when a candidate fails the real type checker, use the proof of untypeability to refine the abstraction — [TYGAR, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04091#page=2), [p.3 (pipeline: initial abstraction → abstract reachability → type checker → refine)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04091#page=3). Evaluation (v1 PDF text): 44 queries over 291 components; "H+ is able to find a well-typed program for 43 out of 44 queries within the timeout of 60 seconds", first well-typed program "within 1.4 seconds on average", and a useful solution in the top five for 32 of 44 — [TYGAR PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04091#page=1). (The arXiv abstract page reads 33 of 44; the PDF text reads 32 — a version discrepancy.)

**Hectare / ECTAs (Koppel, Guo, de Vries, Solar-Lezama, Polikarpova, ICFP 2022)** — "Searching Entangled Program Spaces".
- "We introduce equality-constrained tree automata (ECTAs)… designed to compactly represent large spaces of programs with entangled" subterm choices (type variables shared across argument positions) — [ECTA, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07828#page=1). Typing constraints become equality constraints on automaton paths; *static reduction* (automata intersection along constrained paths, done once before enumeration) and dynamic reduction prune spurious runs — [ECTA, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07828#page=2).
- "Hectare is able to solve 88% of synthesis problems in the combined benchmark suite compared to only 64% by Hoogle+, and on commonly solved benchmarks Hectare is 8× faster on average", "despite its implementation being only a tenth of the size"; ablating either reduction "reduces the number of benchmarks solved" — [ECTA, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07828#page=3).

**Burst (Miltner, Trejo Nuñez, Brendel, Chaudhuri, Dillig, POPL 2022)** — bottom-up synthesis of recursive programs with angelic execution.
- Problem: a recursive subexpression cannot be executed before the whole program exists; solution: "use angelic execution [Broy and Wirsing 1981] to evaluate recursive sub-expressions", then analyze the angelic assumptions and strengthen the spec in a CEGIS-like loop — [Burst, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.06253#page=2). On 45 benchmarks (Myth/Smyth suite) Burst synthesizes "96% (43) of the functions from input/output examples, 96% (43)… from reference implementations, and 91% (41)… from logical formulas"; on logical specs it "solves more benchmarks than both Leon (76%) and Synqid (49%)" — [Burst, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.06253#page=3).

**Combinatory Logic Synthesizer, (CL)S (Rehof's group, TU Dortmund, 2012–2023)** — synthesis literally as type inhabitation.
- "(CL)S is a tool for the automatic composition of software components" that "provides an implementation of a type inhabitation algorithm" for combinatory logic with intersection types; CLS-SMT (2019) couples it to SMT — [CLS-SMT, p.1–2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.09481#page=1).
- Finite Combinatory Logic with Predicates (TYPES 2023): in plain FCL "inhabitant sets correspond to regular tree languages and vice versa", so non-regular properties (e.g. (dis)equality of subterms) cannot be specified; adding decidable predicates makes "type inhabitation in FCLP… undecidable", but "Based on results in tree automata theory, we identify a fragment of FCLP" with decidable inhabitation that still allows local (dis)equality constraints; applied to synthesizing "mechanically sound robotic arms" — [FCLP, p.1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol303-types2023/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2.pdf#page=1), [p.7](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol303-types2023/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2.pdf#page=7).

**Dependent-type proof search in practice.** Agda ships an "Automatic Proof Search (Auto)" tool (the Agsy lineage; the current docs are for Agda 2.9.0) — [Agda docs](https://agda.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tools/auto.html). (No paper on Agsy/Mimer could be fetch-verified; see Gaps.)

### Inferences
- Across Myth, Synquid, Smyth, λ² and Burst, the common pattern is: *decompose top-down while the goal type is informative; enumerate bottom-up only for elimination forms, in normal form, against an inhabitation oracle.* For disp, where a type is a program returning yes/no, the direct analogue of "refinement" is running the recognizer on the partially built tree with holes treated angelically (Burst) or over-approximately (Synquid's round-trip propagation): the recognizer need only be able to reject some partial trees early for the pruning to pay off.
- Synquid's observation that non-terminating programs are "always simpler" than terminating ones is a warning for any "type checker returns 1 × cost score" objective: without a termination side-condition the cost prior will systematically prefer divergent candidates.
- Hoogle+ → Hectare shows the same trend as Neo/Blaze (KQ2): replace one expensive exact check per candidate with a cheap abstract check and a *refine-on-failure* loop. ECTA's "static reduction" is the automata-theoretic version of pruning a hole's candidate stream by equality constraints shared with sibling holes — close to disp's "per-hole candidate streams" plus conflict witnesses, but done ahead of time.
- InSynth (2013) is the earliest verified instance of exactly the project's objective: inhabitation (hard) ranked by additive weights (soft).

### Gaps
- No fetch-verifiable paper on Agda's Agsy (Lindblad & Benke, TYPES 2004) or Mimer; only the docs page was reachable. No system was found that combines a *dependent*-type inhabitation oracle with a *learned* enumeration prior; the closest are (CL)S (intersection types, no learning) and Blinn et al. 2024 (LLM given the hole's type, but the LLM is not an enumerator).
- Myth's per-benchmark timings and example counts were not extracted (only "more than 40 benchmarks").
- Hoogle+ top-5 figure differs between abstract page (33/44) and PDF body (32/44).

## Key question 2: Conflict-driven learning in synthesis (Neo, Trinity, Concord, Blaze/Atlas, learning to prune). How are conflicts generalized into lemmas, and what were the measured speedups?

### Takeaway
Neo (2018) imported CDCL into synthesis: a failing partial program is turned into an SMT formula, its minimal unsatisfiable core names the components responsible, and "equivalence modulo conflict" generalizes the learned lemma to every other component whose specification is interchangeable in that core, so one failure prunes a whole family of programs; Concord (2020) then used the same deduction engine as an off-policy reward signal to update a learned search policy online. Measured gains are large but domain-bound: Neo solved 90% vs Morpheus's 64% (19 s vs 68 s average) on R data-wrangling; Concord solved 15% more than Neo and was 8.71× faster on shared benchmarks; Blaze's abstraction-refinement reported 90× over Prose on matrix tasks. Nothing found in 2024–2026 advances explicit conflict learning further; the recent action is in learned *priors* (KQ3) and LLM-in-the-loop CEGIS (KQ6).

### Cited Findings

**Neo (Feng, Martins, Bastani, Dillig, PLDI 2018).**
- "Given a spurious program that violates the desired specification, our synthesis algorithm identifies the root cause of the conflict and learns new lemmas that can prevent similar mistakes in the future. Specifically, we introduce the notion of equivalence modulo conflict" — [Neo, p.1](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/pldi18-neo.pdf#page=1).
- Mechanism (same PDF): partial programs are checked by conjoining component specifications (first-order formulas over input/output attributes) with the spec; "To identify the root cause of the conflict, Neo starts by computing a minimal unsatisfiable core (MUC) of the formula"; components that are interchangeable in the MUC are "equivalent modulo conflict", and the learned lemma rules out all of them at the same AST positions. The *decide* step uses a statistical model — "a 2-gram model (as used in Morpheus) as well as a deep neural network model (as described in DeepCoder)" — [Neo PDF](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/pldi18-neo.pdf#page=1).
- Results: "Neo can solve 90% of these benchmarks with an average running time of 19 seconds, whereas Morpheus solves 64% with an average running time of 68 seconds"; the ablation "baseline" with depth-first search and a random decider "only solves 10%" — [Neo, p.11](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/pldi18-neo.pdf#page=11). Neo also "outperforms two state-of-the-art synthesis tools, Morpheus and DeepCoder" on their own domains (R data wrangling; list programs with the DeepCoder DSL, against a reimplementation of DeepCoder) — [Neo, p.1](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/pldi18-neo.pdf#page=1).

**Concord (Chen, Wang, Bastani, Dillig, Feng, CAV 2020)** — deduction-guided reinforcement learning.
- "Given an initial policy (i.e. statistical model) trained off-line, our method uses this policy to guide its search", formulates synthesis as an MDP with grammar productions as actions, and "gradually improves the policy by incorporating feedback from a deduction engine"; because a deduction engine's conflict analysis yields "feedback about states that have not been explored" (infeasible programs inferred from one root cause), they "propose an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm" (off-policy policy gradient) — [Concord, p.1–2](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/concord.pdf#page=2).
- Results: "Concord solves 15% more benchmarks compared to Neo, a state-of-the-art synthesis tool, while improving synthesis time by 8.71× on benchmarks that can be solved by both tools" — [Concord, p.1](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/concord.pdf#page=1); the ablation table shows intermediate variants at 1.63×, 3.66×, 2.88× and the full system at 8.71× — [Concord, p.17](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~isil/concord.pdf#page=17).

**Trinity (Martins, Chen, Chen, Feng, Dillig, VLDB 2019 demo).** An extensible framework packaging the Neo-style enumerate–deduce–learn loop with a DSL interpreter and front-end for data-science DSLs — [Trinity, p.1–3](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol12/p1914-martins.pdf#page=1).

**Blaze (Wang, Dillig, Singh, POPL 2018)** — synthesis using abstraction refinement (the abstract-interpretation sibling of conflict learning).
- Workflow: find a program consistent with the examples "with respect to its abstract semantics", check it concretely, and if spurious construct a "proof of incorrectness" that labels AST nodes with abstract values; new predicates from that proof refine the abstraction so the proof "rules out" other spurious programs too — [Blaze, p.1–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.07740#page=1). Results: "compares favorably with FlashFill" on strings and "a 90x speed-up over Prose" on matrix manipulations — [Blaze, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.07740#page=1).

**Atlas (Wang, Anderson, Dillig, McMillan, CAV 2018)** — learning abstractions for synthesis.
- Blaze "expects a domain expert to manually specify a universe of predicate templates, together with sound abstract transformers"; Atlas instead learns "a sequence of increasingly precise abstract domains A1,…,An, and their corresponding abstract transformers" from training problems, "by solving a certain kind of second-order constraint solving problem in a data-driven way" — [Atlas, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.04152#page=1).

**Learned pruning without a conflict analyzer.**
- Probe (Barke, Peleg, Polikarpova, OOPSLA 2020): "instead of training a model ahead of time we show how to bootstrap one just in time, during synthesis, by learning from partial solutions encountered along the way" (a PCFG re-estimated from partially-correct programs) — [Probe, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08663#page=2). (Details and numbers under KQ3.)
- Merlin (Meyer & Rabe?, arXiv Nov 2025): "oriented metrics (orimetrics)" for bottom-up enumeration over asymmetric data domains, run inside a counterexample-guided abstraction refinement loop; reports Merlin is "27 times faster than DryadSynth" on bitvectors — [Merlin, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02491#page=2). (Authors' names beyond the first were not captured; the per-ablation solve counts seen in a search snippet were not found in the PDF text and are omitted.)

### Inferences
- Neo's three ingredients map one-to-one onto the project's optimizer design: *decide* = sample next hole filler from the prior; *deduce* = run the recognizer on the partial tree; *learn* = reify the failure into a lemma. The specific generalization trick — equivalence modulo conflict — requires that components carry *specifications* that are comparable (Neo uses first-order attribute formulas). In disp, where the "specification" of a candidate subtree is the recognizer's verdict at a tree position with an expected type, the analogue is: two candidate subtrees are equivalent modulo this conflict if the recognizer's rejection trace does not depend on how they differ. That is what Neo's MUC computes in the SMT setting; a step-budgeted recognizer could compute it by replay with the subtree abstracted.
- Concord's lesson is that conflicts are worth more as *training signal* than as individual pruning lemmas: the lemma prunes once, the policy update prunes forever (for that distribution).
- Blaze/Atlas show the dual approach: instead of learning *what to avoid* (lemmas), learn *which abstraction to check with* (predicates/transformers). For a yes/no recognizer, the natural abstraction is a cheaper, over-approximating recognizer (fewer steps), refined when it accepts something the full recognizer rejects.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 paper was found that extends explicit conflict-driven learning (MUC + lemma) beyond Neo/Concord; the only recent "learning to prune" items are PCFG/metric-based (Probe, Merlin). This absence is itself a finding: the community moved to learned priors and LLM proposals.
- Neo's DeepCoder-domain numbers (solve counts/times) were not extracted; only the qualitative "outperforms" and the Morpheus-domain numbers are cited.

## Key question 3: Learned priors over enumeration (DeepCoder, Euphony, Probe, Bustle, CrossBeam, LambdaBeam, TF-Coder, NGDS). How is the prior combined with exhaustive search?

### Takeaway
There are four well-tested ways to splice a learned prior into an exhaustive enumerator: (1) *reorder a complete search* (DeepCoder's sort-and-add DFS; Euphony's A* over a probabilistic grammar; Heap Search), which keeps completeness and gives order-of-magnitude speedups; (2) *reweight bottom-up enumeration* so cheaper-looking expressions are built first (TF-Coder per-operation weights, Bustle's property-signature model, Probe's just-in-time PCFG, Bee Search's cost-tuple best-first); (3) *replace the combinatorial step with a learned policy* that picks which explored values to combine (CrossBeam, LambdaBeam), trading completeness for 20× fewer candidates; (4) *gate a deductive decomposition with a learned branch scorer* (NGDS's branch-and-bound over PROSE witness functions). Fijalkow et al. (2022) proved that ordering by probability ("loss-optimal") is the right target for approach (1) and that both A* (Euphony) and Heap Search achieve it. Since 2024 the prior is increasingly an LLM: Li–Parsert–Polgreen (CAV 2024) convert LLM guesses into a pCFG for weighted enumeration and beat cvc5, and type-constrained decoding (PLDI 2025) runs the type checker as a hard mask inside the LLM's own sampling loop.

### Cited Findings

**DeepCoder (Balog et al., ICLR 2017).**
- "train a neural network to predict properties of the program that generated the outputs from the inputs. We use the neural network's predictions to augment search" — [DeepCoder, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.01989#page=1). The search variants are DFS, "Sort and add" (DFS restricted to a growing active set ordered by predicted probability), Sketch, and beam; Lemma 1 bounds expected sort-and-add runtime by marginal probabilities; Table 1 reports speedups on length-3 programs of 15.2× to 94.6× depending on search (same PDF).

**RobustFill (Devlin et al., ICML 2017)** — purely neural seq2seq decoding with beam search under noisy I/O — [RobustFill, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.07469#page=1).

**Neural-Guided Deductive Search, NGDS (Vijayakumar/Kalyan, Mohta, Polozov, Batra, Jain, Gulwani, ICLR 2018).**
- Built "upon the deductive search of the PROSE system, which uses symbolic PL insights in the form of witness functions to construct and narrow down the search space, and a ranking function h to pick the most generalizable program" — [NGDS, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.01186#page=7). A "branch & bound" controller uses a neural model to predict, a priori, the generalization score of the best program in a branch and skips branches that cannot beat the incumbent; reported "up to 12× speed-up compared to state-of-the-art systems" and single-example generalization on 68% of scenarios vs 24–36% for baselines — [NGDS, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.01186#page=1), [p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.01186#page=3).
- FlashMeta (Polozov & Gulwani, OOPSLA 2015) is the deductive substrate: it "propagates example-based constraints on an expression to its subexpressions by leveraging associated witness functions, which essentially capture the inverse semantics of the underlying operator" and combines "deductive inference and enumerative search" — [FlashMeta, p.1](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/oopsla15-pbe.pdf#page=1), [p.4](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/oopsla15-pbe.pdf#page=4).

**Euphony (Lee, Heo, Alur, Naik, PLDI 2018)** — learned probabilistic models for SyGuS.
- Learns a probabilistic higher-order grammar (PHOG) by "transfer learning" from solutions of easier problems and enumerates "programs in order of their likelihood" — [Euphony, p.1](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/PLDI18.pdf#page=1); the enumerator is "A* search over the derivation graph of sentential forms" (weighted enumerative search, with a divide-and-conquer variant) — [Euphony, p.3](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/PLDI18.pdf#page=3).
- Results: evaluated on 1,167 SyGuS benchmarks; on the 405 test problems Euphony solves 236 vs EUSolver's 87; "A*+PHOG, Uniform+PHOG, A*+PCFG, and Uniform+PCFG are able to solve 236, 209, 133, 22 instances, respectively… PHOG significantly outperforms PCFG, and A* outperforms uniform search" — [Euphony, p.12](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/PLDI18.pdf#page=12); vs the domain-specific FlashFill, Euphony "outperforms FlashFill on 20 out of 22 synthesis problems and is 10x faster on average" — [Euphony, p.2](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/PLDI18.pdf#page=2).
- PHOG itself (Bielik, Raychev, Vechev, ICML 2016): "generalizes probabilistic context free grammars (PCFGs) by allowing conditioning of a production rule beyond the parent nonterminal" via a learned context program — [PHOG, p.1](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/bielik16.pdf#page=1), [p.4](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/bielik16.pdf#page=4).

**Probe (Barke, Peleg, Polikarpova, OOPSLA 2020)** — just-in-time learning for bottom-up enumeration.
- Learns a PCFG during synthesis from partial solutions (programs that satisfy a subset of examples) and uses it in "guided bottom-up search" — [Probe, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08663#page=2). Results: Probe solves 91 of the 140 SyGuS problems within ten minutes "compared to only 44 problems for the baseline bottom-up synthesizer and 50 problems for EuPhony"; CVC4 solves more but "CVC4 achieves only 68% accuracy on unseen data" because its solutions overfit — [Probe, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08663#page=3).

**Bustle (Odena, Shi, Bieber, Singh, Sutton, Dai, ICLR 2021).**
- Bottom-up enumeration (TRANSIT-style, smallest to largest, storing *values* so observationally-equivalent expressions are merged) with a model that re-weights intermediate expressions using "property signatures" computed on the concrete values — [Bustle, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14381#page=1), [p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14381#page=4).

**CrossBeam (Shi, Dai, Ellis, Sutton, ICLR 2022)** — learning to search in bottom-up synthesis.
- "we propose training a neural model to learn a hands-on search policy for bottom-up synthesis, instead of relying on a combinatorial search algorithm" — [CrossBeam, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10452#page=1); the policy predicts argument lists as pointers into the table of explored values, trained on-policy by imitation; "CrossBeam solves 62% more tasks than Bustle" and "considers about 20× fewer candidate programs" to match Bustle at 50K candidates; in inductive logic programming it reaches "nearly 100% success rate on tasks with large enough solutions that prior state-of-the-art has a 0% success rate" — [CrossBeam, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10452#page=2), [p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10452#page=6).

**LambdaBeam (Shi, Dai, Ellis, Sutton, NeurIPS 2023).**
- Constructs "arbitrary lambda functions that compose operations within a given DSL", represents each lambda by a "semantic vector representation of the execution behavior", and trains "a neural policy network to choose which lambdas to construct during search"; "LambdaBeam outperforms neural, symbolic, and LLM-based techniques in an integer list manipulation domain" (100 handwritten + 100 synthetic tasks, 10-minute limit) — [LambdaBeam, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.02049#page=1).
- AbstractBeam (Zenkner, Dierkes, Sesterhenn, Bartelt, 2024): adds library learning to LambdaBeam and "statistically significantly (p < 0.05) outperforms LambdaBeam in the integer list manipulation domain" — [AbstractBeam, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.17514#page=1).

**TF-Coder (Shi, Bieber, Singh, TOPLAS 2022).**
- "TF-Coder uses a bottom-up weighted enumerative search, with value-based pruning of equivalent expressions and flexible type- and value-based filtering", plus two learned models that predict useful operations "from features of the input and output tensors and natural language descriptions" to set per-operation weights; "solves 63 of 70 real-world tasks within 5 minutes" (Transit solves 39) — [TF-Coder, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.09040#page=1).

**Distribution-based search (Fijalkow, Lagarde, Matricon, Ellis, Ohlmann, Potta, AAAI 2022)** — the theory of "enumerate by prior".
- Defines the *loss* of a search algorithm as "the expected number of programs" enumerated before the solution under the predicted distribution; an algorithm is "loss optimal" iff it generates each program once in non-increasing probability order; "Theorem 1. The Heap Search algorithm is loss optimal", and "Heap Search is related to A* from Feng et al. (2018) [Euphony]: they are both loss optimal algorithms"; Theorem 2 gives a dichotomy for sampling (SQRT Sampling is loss optimal among sampling algorithms when Σ√D(x) < ∞); a "grammar splitter" partitions a PCFG so parallel workers never generate the same program — [Fijalkow et al., p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12485#page=2), [p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12485#page=4), [p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12485#page=6).

**Bee Search (Ameen & Lelis, JAIR 2023).** Cost-guided bottom-up algorithms "round off the costs of the programs. As a result, they do not necessarily perform the search in best-first order"; Bee Search searches "an abstract space of program costs" (cost tuples) so it is best-first and never materializes programs costlier than the solution — [Bee Search, p.2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04327#page=2).

**Sketch-level priors and execution-guided neural search.**
- Nye, Hewitt, Tenenbaum, Solar-Lezama (ICML 2019): a neural model emits a sketch with `<HOLE>` tokens and a symbolic enumerator fills the holes — [Learning to Infer Program Sketches, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.06349#page=3).
- Ellis, Nye, Pu et al. (NeurIPS 2019): policy + value network + REPL combined with Sequential Monte Carlo at inference — [Write, Execute, Assess, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.04604#page=2).
- Austin et al. (2021) established the LLM-only baseline for program synthesis from natural language (MBPP) — [Program Synthesis with LLMs, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.07732#page=1).
- The "Neurosymbolic Programming" monograph (Chaudhuri, Ellis, Polozov, Singh, Solar-Lezama, Yue, 2021) categorizes "the main ways in which symbolic and neural learning techniques come together" — [Neurosymbolic Programming, p.2](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~swarat/pubs/PGL-049-Plain.pdf#page=2).

**LLM as prior for an exhaustive enumerator (2024–2026).**
- Li, Parsert, Polgreen (CAV 2024): prompting alone "enables us to solve roughly 50% of the SyGuS competition" benchmarks (49% overall); *pCFG-synth* builds a probabilistic CFG from the LLM's (often wrong) guesses and weights an enumerator with it; *iLLM-synth* queries the LLM during enumeration with partial enumerations, failed candidates and counterexamples, and "outperforms cvc5, the state-of-the-art synthesizer, as well as our baseline enumerators" — [Li et al., p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03997#page=3), [p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03997#page=5); the integrated method beats cvc5 "solving 73 more benchmarks" — [Li et al., p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03997#page=16).

**Hard constraints inside LLM decoding (2022–2025).**
- Synchromesh (Poesia et al., ICLR 2022): "Constrained Semantic Decoding (CSD): a general framework for constraining the output to a set of valid programs" by querying a completion engine at every token — [Synchromesh, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.11227#page=1), [p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.11227#page=4).
- Monitor-Guided Decoding (Agrawal, Kanade, Goyal, Lahiri, Rajamani, NeurIPS 2023): "a monitor uses static analysis to guide the decoding" by masking type-inconsistent identifiers; "SantaCoder-1.1B achieves better compilation rate and next-identifier match than the much larger text-davinci-003" — [MGD, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10763#page=1).
- Grammar-Aligned Decoding (Park, Wang, Berg-Kirkpatrick, Polikarpova, D'Antoni, NeurIPS 2024): greedy grammar-constrained decoding "distorts the LLM's learned language distribution"; GAD is the problem of sampling exactly from the LLM conditioned on the grammar, and ASAp approximates it by learning expected futures — [GAD, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.21047#page=1), [p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.21047#page=2).
- Type-Constrained Code Generation (Mündler, He, Wang, Sen, Song, Vechev, PLDI 2025): "we develop novel prefix automata and a search over inhabitable types, forming a sound approach to enforce well-typedness on LLM-generated code" — [Mündler et al., p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09246#page=1), [prefix automaton definition p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09246#page=9); motivation: "94% of compilation errors result from failing type checks" in generated TypeScript; result: "reduces compilation errors by more than half and increases functional correctness relatively by 3.5% to 5.5%" and improves functionally correct repair "by 37%" — [Mündler et al., p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09246#page=2).

### Inferences
- The project's "candidate streams ordered by a prior (size, library frequency, LLM logits)" is exactly Fijalkow's distribution-based search; the theorem says the stream should be *loss-optimal* — generate each candidate once in non-increasing probability — which Heap Search does with a per-hole heap over the PCFG. Any bucketing of the prior into cost classes (TF-Coder-style integer weights) forfeits best-first order, which is Bee Search's complaint.
- Euphony's PHOG>PCFG result (236 vs 133 solved) is the strongest single data point that *context-conditioned* priors (what is the parent, what are the siblings) matter more than the search algorithm; a disp prior conditioned on the hole's expected type is the natural next step beyond PHOG's syntactic context.
- CrossBeam/LambdaBeam show the extreme end: let the policy *be* the enumerator. The cost is losing the completeness guarantee that makes conflict lemmas sound; a hybrid (policy orders, checker prunes, lemmas restrict) is what Concord did.
- Mündler et al. is the closest published analogue to "type checker returns 1 as a hard constraint while an LLM supplies the soft score", and it already needs a "search over inhabitable types" — a tiny type-inhabitation oracle invoked at each decoding step — to decide which tokens cannot lead to a well-typed program. GAD's warning applies to disp if the LLM prior is used as a *distribution* rather than a ranking: masking changes the effective distribution.

### Gaps
- CrossBeam/LambdaBeam exact task counts beyond the quoted percentages were not extracted. LambdaBeam's comparison numbers against CrossBeam specifically were not found in the text searched.
- Bustle's own solved-task counts were not extracted (only mechanism).
- No verified source compares learned-prior enumeration against conflict-driven learning on the same benchmarks except Concord vs Neo.

## Key question 4: Library learning and wake-sleep (DreamCoder, Stitch, babble, LILO, and 2024–2026 successors). How do learned abstractions change the search space?

### Takeaway
DreamCoder (2021) made library learning a Bayesian loop: wake = search for programs under the current library, guided by a neural recognition model; sleep-abstraction = refactor found programs to extract reusable lambdas into the library (the generative prior); sleep-dreaming = retrain the recognizer on programs sampled from the prior. Stitch (POPL 2023) made the abstraction step 3–4 orders of magnitude faster by synthesizing abstractions top-down with branch-and-bound on a compression utility, and babble (POPL 2023) made it precise with e-graphs plus anti-unification; LILO (ICLR 2024) swapped the enumerator for an LLM and showed the library helps the LLM only when abstractions are *named and documented*. The 2024–2026 record is mixed: ReGAL/Leroy/AbstractBeam report gains, but a 2024 audit found that two celebrated LLM "library learners" almost never reuse what they learn, and 2026 work (DreamProver, "prospective compression") argues the compression objective should target *future* tasks rather than retrospectively compress past ones.

### Cited Findings

**DreamCoder (Ellis et al., PLDI 2021; Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A 2023).**
- "DreamCoder, a system that learns to solve problems by writing programs. It builds expertise by creating programming languages for expressing domain concepts, together with neural networks to guide the search for programs within these languages. A 'wake-sleep' learning algorithm alternately extends the language with new symbolic abstractions" and trains the network — [DreamCoder, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.08381#page=1). Wake: "recognition model which ranks candidate programs"; sleep-abstraction "grows the library of programming primitives (the generative model)" by refactoring solutions; sleep-dreaming trains the recognition model Q(ρ|x) on "fantasies" sampled from the generative model, "in the spirit of the Helmholtz machine"; applied to "eight domains"; on a SyGuS text-editing suite it solves 3.7% of problems within 10 minutes before learning and 84.3% with a generous budget after — [DreamCoder PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.08381#page=1).

**Stitch (Bowers, Olausson, Wong, Grand, Tenenbaum, Ellis, Solar-Lezama, POPL 2023).**
- "corpus-guided top-down synthesis as a mechanism for synthesizing library functions that capture common functionality from a corpus of programs"; it is "a novel, strongly-guided branch-and-bound algorithm for synthesizing function abstractions" where the utility of expanding a partial abstraction is upper-bounded by its best possible compression — [Stitch, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.16605#page=1), [p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.16605#page=3). "Stitch is 3-4 orders of magnitude faster and uses 2 orders of magnitude less memory while maintaining comparable or better library quality (as measured by compressivity)" than DreamCoder's compressor; test-set compression ratios of 2.55×–11.57× in 0.19–60.16 s — [Stitch, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.16605#page=1).

**babble (Cao, Kunkel, Nandi, Willsey, Tatlock, Polikarpova, POPL 2023).**
- Library learning "with E-Graphs and Anti-Unification": equality saturation puts semantically equivalent program variants into one e-graph so that abstractions common up to rewriting are found, and anti-unification over the e-graph generates candidate abstractions precisely — [babble, p.2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04596#page=2).

**LILO (Grand, Wong, Bowers, Olausson, Liu, Tenenbaum, Andreas, ICLR 2024).**
- "a neurosymbolic framework that iteratively synthesizes, compresses, and documents code to build libraries tailored to particular problem domains. LILO combines LLM-guided program synthesis" with Stitch compression and an AutoDoc step that names and documents abstractions — [LILO, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.19791#page=1); it "solves more tasks than DreamCoder and learns empirically richer libraries" — [LILO, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.19791#page=2). Key negative finding in the same PDF: "naively providing a LLM with Stitch abstractions measurably degraded its ability" to use them, motivating AutoDoc; on one domain the best baseline solve rate was 97.09% and LILO brought it to 99.03% — [LILO PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.19791#page=2).

**2024 successors.**
- ReGAL (Stengel-Eskin, Prasad, Bansal, ICML 2024): an LLM refactors solved programs into helper functions; "For CodeLlama-13B, ReGAL results in absolute accuracy increases of 11.5% on LOGO, 26.1% on date understanding, and 8.1% on TextCraft" — [ReGAL, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.16467#page=1).
- Leroy (Bellur, Alghamdi, et al., 2024): library learning for imperative Python by "lispifying" ASTs so Stitch-style compression applies — [Leroy, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06438#page=1), [p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06438#page=4).
- AbstractBeam (2024): see KQ3 — library learning added to LambdaBeam's neural bottom-up search — [AbstractBeam, p.2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.17514#page=2).

**Negative result (Berlot-Attwell et al., Oct 2024) — "Library Learning Doesn't: The Curious Case of the Single-Use 'Library'".**
- Studying LEGO-Prover (Isabelle lemmas) and TroVE (Python tools), both of which "reported increased accuracy": "function reuse is extremely infrequent on miniF2F and MATH… our analysis of the model's behaviour reveals that direct reuse is negligible", and ablations "suggest that, rather than reuse, self-correction and self-consistency are the" sources of the gains — [Library Learning Doesn't, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20274#page=1).

**2025–2026.**
- A comparative review (Kobaladze, Arnania, Sanikidze, Aug 2025) still calls DreamCoder "the seminal system in this domain" for library learning — [survey, p.59](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00013#page=59).
- DreamProver (Zhang, Sun, Bi, Geng, Ma et al., Apr 2026, preprint): "Inspired by the wake-sleep algorithm (Hinton et al., 1995; Ellis et al., 2021), DreamProver operates in iterative wake–sleep cycles" to evolve a transferable *lemma* library for an LLM theorem-proving agent; ablations remove "iterative wake-sleep refinement, and cluster-based lemma evolution" — [DreamProver, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.26311#page=2), [p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.26311#page=8).
- "Prospective Compression in Human Abstraction Learning" (May 2026, preprint): frames "online library learning: the incremental acquisition of reusable abstractions under uncertainty about future task demands" and argues "Existing library learning algorithms compress past tasks retrospectively" whereas abstractions should be chosen to compress *future* tasks — [Prospective Compression, p.1–2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09985#page=1).

### Inferences
- Library learning changes the search space in two measurable ways: it shortens solutions (so a size-ordered enumerator reaches them sooner — DreamCoder's 3.7% → 84.3%), and it changes the *prior* (library frequency becomes a feature). For disp's "library frequency" prior, Stitch's compression utility is the right thing to maximize when deciding which abstraction to admit, and babble's anti-unification over an e-graph is the right candidate generator when candidates are equal modulo rewriting (which, for tree-calculus programs, they often will be).
- The LILO AutoDoc finding and the Berlot-Attwell audit together say: a library only helps a *learned* proposer if the proposer can recognize when to use it. For a symbolic enumerator the library helps unconditionally (shorter programs); for an LLM prior it helps only with names/docstrings, and claimed gains must be checked for actual reuse.
- The 2026 "prospective compression" critique applies directly to an optimizer that learns abstractions from its own trace: compressing the past can overfit to already-solved holes.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 system was found that is a direct DreamCoder successor in program synthesis (as opposed to theorem proving); DreamProver and the prospective-compression paper are the nearest.
- LILO's per-domain solve-rate tables beyond the one 97.09/99.03 figure were not extracted.

## Key question 5: Complexity of the oracle (inhabitation PSPACE-complete for STLC; undecidable for System F, Fω, dependent types; consequences for synthesis)

### Takeaway
Type inhabitation — "is there a closed term of this type?" — is PSPACE-complete for the simply typed λ-calculus (Statman 1979), undecidable for System F and Fω (Löb 1976, by embedding first-order predicate logic into second-order propositional intuitionistic logic; Urzyczyn 1997 gave direct syntactic proofs), and undecidable for the dependent calculus λP because λP is conservative over first-order intuitionistic logic, which is undecidable even in its ∀/→ fragment. Even the finite, monomorphic combinatory setting is EXPTIME-complete, and allowing k levels of polymorphic instantiation makes it (k+2)-EXPTIME-complete; the relativized problem (arbitrary axiom schemes, i.e. an arbitrary component library with polymorphic types) is undecidable even for simple types. Synthesis systems live with this by restricting the type universe (finite component sets, monomorphic or bounded instantiation, abstract types), by bounding depth/size/steps, and by using refutation-first checks (abstract semantics, deduction, counterexamples) that are cheap to run and only sound in the rejecting direction.

### Cited Findings
- Urzyczyn's own announcement (TYPES list, 1996) of the TLCA 1997 paper: "the inhabitation problem is PSPACE-complete for simply typed lambda-calculus and undecidable for the polymorphic second-order and higher-order lambda calculi (systems F and F-omega)", with "entirely syntactic" proofs — [Urzyczyn, TYPES list post](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~bcpierce/types/archives/1996/msg00387.html).
- Statman's result as stated in the Curry–Howard lecture notes (Sørensen & Urzyczyn; hosted PDF): "a result of Statman [103], that the inhabitation problem for the finitely-typed lambda calculus is PSPACE-complete. In particular it is decidable. An immediate consequence is that provability in IPC(→) is also" decidable — [Lectures, p.111](https://disi.unitn.it/~bernardi/RSISE11/Papers/curry-howard.pdf#page=111); the proof is "by a reduction from the satisfiability problem for classical second-order propositional formulas" — [Lectures, p.4](https://disi.unitn.it/~bernardi/RSISE11/Papers/curry-howard.pdf#page=4).
- System F: the inhabitation problem for the second-order λ-calculus "('Given type τ, is there a closed term of type τ?') is undecidable" — [Lectures, p.210](https://disi.unitn.it/~bernardi/RSISE11/Papers/curry-howard.pdf#page=210); the source theorem is Löb (JSL 1976), which studies "intuitionistic second order propositional logic (ISPL)" and embeds first-order predicate logic into it — [Löb 1976, abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-symbolic-logic/article/abs/embedding-first-order-predicate-logic-in-fragments-of-intuitionistic-logic/355D43082A68EBDF20EDEE0D50D4A167#sec0).
- Dependent types: "As we have already observed, first-order intuitionistic logic is undecidable. Thus type inhabitation in the corresponding fragment of λP must be also undecidable. But λP can be shown conservative over this fragment, and it follows that type inhabitation for the whole system is also undecidable. 10.6.2. Theorem. Type inhabitation in λP is undecidable." — [Lectures, p.177](https://disi.unitn.it/~bernardi/RSISE11/Papers/curry-howard.pdf#page=177). The same notes record that first-order intuitionistic logic is undecidable "already for a very restricted fragment… with ∀ and → as the only connectives" (Corollary 9.3.8) and that even *type checking* in Curry-style λP is undecidable (Dowek) — [Lectures PDF](https://disi.unitn.it/~bernardi/RSISE11/Papers/curry-howard.pdf#page=177).
- Combinatory/component setting (Düdder, Martens, Rehof, Urzyczyn, CSL 2012): for a fixed combinator basis "the set of provable formulas (inhabited types) is PSPACE-complete in simple types and undecidable in intersection types. When arbitrary sets of axiom schemes are considered, the inhabitation problem is undecidable even in simple types" (Linial–Post); for k-bounded combinatory logic "the problem is (k + 2)-EXPTIME complete for bcl_k with intersection types" and "EXPTIME-complete for simple types, for all k"; a "nonelementary lower bound follows for the problem where k is taken as an input parameter"; the monomorphic (k=0) problem is EXPTIME-complete — [BCL, p.1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol016-csl2012/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243.pdf#page=1), [p.3](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol016-csl2012/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243.pdf#page=3). The motivation given is that bounding instantiation makes "inhabitation… decidable in cases where the unbounded problem is undecidable" — [BCL, p.2](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol016-csl2012/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243/LIPIcs.CSL.2012.243.pdf#page=2).
- FCLP (TYPES 2023) is the newest step on this frontier: adding decidable predicates to finite combinatory logic makes inhabitation undecidable; a tree-automata fragment restores decidability — [FCLP, p.1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol303-types2023/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2/LIPIcs.TYPES.2023.2.pdf#page=1).
- Synthesis-side acknowledgement: ReSyn states that for recursive programs "both synthesis and resource analysis are undecidable in theory and expensive in practice", which is why it integrates the resource check into type-directed search rather than running it after — [ReSyn, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07415#page=1).
- CEGIS-side theory (Jha & Seshia, Acta Informatica 2017): "When the concept class is infinite size, termination of CEGIS is not guaranteed"; with unbounded memory, learners given arbitrary vs minimal counterexamples have the same synthesis power; for finite concept classes the problem is NP-hard for a large class of oracle-guided techniques and convergence speed relates to teaching dimension — [Jha & Seshia, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.03953#page=3), [p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.03953#page=1).
- How systems respond (all from KQ1–KQ3 sources): Hoogle+ searches over *abstract* types and refines only on a type-check failure — [TYGAR, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04091#page=2); Hectare encodes types as automata constraints and reduces statically — [ECTA, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07828#page=2); Synquid requires a termination proof for every fixpoint and uses an SMT-backed refinement logic that is decidable by construction (liquid types) — [Synquid, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.08419#page=2); Myth enumerates only β-normal forms, exploiting that "the ill-typed terms vastly outnumber the well-typed terms" — [Myth, p.1](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~stevez/papers/OZ15.pdf#page=1); Mündler et al. keep LLM decoding sound by searching only over *inhabitable* types reachable from the current context — [Mündler et al., p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09246#page=1).

### Inferences
- For disp the oracle is not "is this type inhabited" but "does this recognizer program return 1 on this candidate", which is at least as hard (the recognizer is an arbitrary program; running it is Turing-complete). The literature's three coping strategies translate directly: (i) a step budget (the project already has this) is the analogue of bounded instantiation/depth; (ii) refutation-first means the search should treat "returns 0 within budget" as a hard prune and "did not finish" as merely a lower prior, never as acceptance; (iii) restricting candidates to a finite, well-typed component library (Hoogle+/BCL style) is what makes any completeness or lemma-soundness claim possible.
- The (k+2)-EXPTIME tower for bounded polymorphism is a quantitative reason to keep the *type* side of the search shallow (monomorphic instances, few universals) and push expressiveness into the recognizer program instead, where cost is paid per step rather than per decision.
- Jha–Seshia's result that minimal counterexamples do not increase power (with unbounded memory) suggests that for a disp conflict witness, *where* in the tree the recognizer failed matters less in principle than *that* it failed — but with bounded memory (a finite lemma store) minimal/structured witnesses do help, which is the regime a real optimizer is in.

### Gaps
- Statman's 1979 paper and Urzyczyn's 1997 TLCA paper could only be verified by metadata (Elsevier redirect; Springer bot wall); their results are cited through the lecture notes and Urzyczyn's own mailing-list post. Löb 1976 is verified only via the Cambridge abstract page.
- No source was found giving complexity bounds for inhabitation in *refinement* type systems with decidable refinement logics (Liquid Haskell/Synquid-style); Synquid's decidability comes from the liquid-type restriction, but no bound was located.
- No verified source quantifies how often real synthesis tasks hit the undecidable regime versus a bounded one.

## Key question 6: CEGIS and sketching (Solar-Lezama; SyGuS) as the counterexample-driven version of the loop, plus LLM proposals combined with CEGIS (2023–2026)

### Takeaway
CEGIS (Solar-Lezama, 2008) is the same propose–check–learn loop with the roles filled by an inductive synthesizer (SAT over a sketch's holes), a verifier, and counterexample *inputs* as the learned constraint; its justification is an explicit empirical hypothesis that a handful of inputs pins down the program. SyGuS (2013) standardized the problem as "find f in grammar G satisfying spec φ in theory T" and spawned the competition that Euphony, Probe, cvc5 and the 2024 LLM-guided enumerators all run on. Since 2023, LLMs have been slotted into the CEGIS learner position in three ways: as the learner itself with a SAT/SMT verifier (Jha et al. 2023, planning), as a prior that seeds a weighted enumerator and is re-prompted with counterexamples (Li–Parsert–Polgreen 2024, beats cvc5), and as a repair loop fed property-violation counterexamples with early-stopped evaluation (Pereira et al. 2026). Type systems entered the LLM loop as token-level hard constraints in 2023–2025 (MGD, type-constrained decoding).

### Cited Findings
- Sketching/CEGIS (Solar-Lezama, PhD thesis 2008): "a new counterexample guided inductive synthesis procedure (CEGIS) which combines the inductive synthesizer with a validation procedure" — [Sketch thesis, p.4](https://people.csail.mit.edu/asolar/papers/thesis.pdf#page=4); "Counterexample guided inductive synthesis is based on an important empirical hypothesis" that a small set of inputs suffices to determine the solution; if the candidate is incorrect "the counterexample produced by the validation procedure is fed to the inductive synthesizer" — [Sketch thesis, p.24](https://people.csail.mit.edu/asolar/papers/thesis.pdf#page=24).
- SyGuS (Alur, Bodik, Juniwal, Martin, Raghothaman, Seshia, Singh, Solar-Lezama, Torlak, Udupa, FMCAD 2013): the problem is defined as "Given a background theory T, a typed function symbol f, a formula φ… and a set of expressions" (a grammar), find an expression for f that satisfies φ; the paper gives "a generic architecture for solving the proposed problem" by counterexample-guided search — [SyGuS, p.3](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/SyGuS13.pdf#page=3), [p.2](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/SyGuS13.pdf#page=2).
- Euphony explicitly frames its learned-prior enumeration as "guiding search-based synthesis using the CEGIS procedure" — [Euphony PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~alur/PLDI18.pdf#page=3); Burst, Smyth and Synquid-style tools likewise accept non-example specs by wrapping the example-based core in a CEGIS loop — [Burst, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.06253#page=2).
- Theory: Jha & Seshia (2017) place CEGIS inside "oracle-guided inductive synthesis (OGIS)" and prove termination is not guaranteed for infinite concept classes — [Jha & Seshia, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.03953#page=1), [p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.03953#page=3).
- LLM as CEGIS learner (Jha, Jha, Lincoln, Bastian, Velasquez, Ewetz, Neema, Sept 2023): plans/programs proposed by an LLM are checked by a satisfiability solver whose counterexamples are appended to the prompt, so "our approach becomes an instance of counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS)" — [Jha et al. 2023, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.16436#page=4).
- LLM as prior for an enumerative CEGIS solver (Li, Parsert, Polgreen, CAV 2024): see KQ3 — the LLM is prompted with "partially enumerated functions, incorrect solutions, and counterexamples" during search and the combination beats cvc5 by 73 benchmarks — [Li et al., p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03997#page=5), [p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03997#page=16).
- Property-guided LLM synthesis for planning heuristics (Pereira, Corrêa, Seipp, May 2026, preprint): "When the property is violated, we stop the evaluation early and provide the LLM with a concrete counterexample showing exactly how the program failed. This feedback drastically reduces both the number of" candidates and evaluation time; the pipeline is "a single counterexample-driven repair loop" — [Pereira et al., p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16142#page=1), [p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16142#page=4).
- Hard type/semantic constraints inside LLM decoding as the token-granular version of CEGIS's verifier: Synchromesh's completion-engine constraints (2022) — [Synchromesh, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.11227#page=1); Monitor-Guided Decoding's static-analysis monitors (2023) — [MGD, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10763#page=1); type-constrained decoding's prefix automata and inhabitable-type search (PLDI 2025) — [Mündler et al., p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09246#page=1); and GAD's proof that greedy masking biases the sampled distribution (2024) — [GAD, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.21047#page=1).

### Inferences
- CEGIS's "counterexample" is a learned constraint on *inputs*; Neo's lemma is a learned constraint on *programs*; disp's conflict witness (position, expected type, actual type) is a constraint on *program–type pairs*. All three are instances of Jha–Seshia's oracle-guided inductive synthesis, which means their termination theory (no guarantee for infinite classes; NP-hard for finite) carries over.
- The 2023–2026 LLM+CEGIS papers converge on the same engineering shape as the project's design: the LLM is a *proposer re-prompted with the structured failure*, never a verifier. The empirical lesson from Li et al. is that the proposals are more valuable as a *distribution* feeding a complete enumerator (pCFG-synth/iLLM-synth) than as direct answers (49% alone).
- Pereira et al.'s early-stopped evaluation on first property violation is the planning-domain analogue of a step-budgeted recognizer that stops at the first `0`.

### Gaps
- No verified 2024–2026 work was found combining LLM proposals with CEGIS for *dependently typed* or refinement-typed targets; the LLM+CEGIS literature found is for SyGuS theories (LIA, bitvectors, invariants) and planning heuristics.
- No verified source measured how many CEGIS iterations LLM proposers need relative to enumerators on the same SyGuS benchmarks beyond Li et al.'s totals.
