Running backward

Drag the bar at the bottom to scrub through the run, either way.

The program is one tree of leaves, stems and forks, the same agents any data is made of, and it never changes while it runs. A token walks it, carrying the tree being worked on and a stack.

Nothing here says what the program means. What a node does is found out when the token reaches it and a rule fires there: the rule's words flash over the node, and the node keeps a ring in the colour of what it did (blue rearrange, purple copy or choose, green work on a part, orange call). Run it back and the rings come off again.

A call copies nothing: the token goes to the definition's node, leaving on its stack where to come back to (the dashed line). Calling a definition backward walks the same tree backward.

Agents are made only on special occasions: when the tree being worked on grows, when a shared copy is taken apart, and for a frame on the stack. A rearrangement reuses the agents it took apart.

As an interaction net of raw trees, every agent a leaf, a stem or a fork and every move spelled out as meetings of two of them: inet.html.

Tokens split and come back together. At a both a token becomes a fork: the pair it carried waits beside the node (green), with a hole for each half, and a token of its own takes each half (a dashed wire from its hole to it). A half done goes back into its hole; with both back, the fork is one token again.

No order is needed. Every move is undone by another, and moves of different tokens touch different agents, so tokens may move in any order and nothing records which went when. "A round at a time" (R) moves everything that can move at a round's start; unticked, one moves at a time. Going back, the halves do not wait for each other as they did coming, so the way back can differ from the way there; it ends at the same start. The bar: the run's moves, each round as wide as its moves and as high as how many tokens there were.

Two-way programs are trees, as everything here is: an instruction is a node, its parts are its children (and as data each is an ordinary tree). The token walks the program tree: down into an instruction, through its parts in order, back up, carrying the tree being worked on (beside it).

The instructions lose nothing: rearrange takes the tree apart by one pattern and puts the same pieces together by another; copy's inverse takes back a copy only from two equal halves; on works on one part while the rest waits parked beside the instruction; then, choose (by patterns, the arms giving different shapes) and loop; call lays a copy of a definition out underneath while the token is inside; run runs a program that arrives as data. So every program has an inverse read off its text: add' subtracts, and dagger, itself a two-way program, works inverses out on programs as trees.

Running backward is turning the token around: it walks back the way it came, undoing each step. Nothing is recorded anywhere.

The tree calculus is a library program, tc: a tree applied to a tree, with a trace handed back beside the answer. Its K rule is a rearrangement putting the dropped argument on the trace; its S rule copies the argument and makes three applications; triage puts the arms it does not take on the trace. So disp's programs run here, and back: tc add 1 1.

What to do with the trace is a choice: keep it (everything runs back), clean it up (bennett: run, copy the answer, run back; twice the steps, and what is left is the program, its arguments and the answer), or throw it away (forget, the one step that cannot be undone). A program written two-way has no trace to begin with.

The bar under the program: how many definitions are laid out after each step (amber) and how many tree nodes are held (blue line).

The tree-calculus interaction net (the strand lattice's rules), run so that every step can be taken back exactly. Back at the start, every agent is in its place with its own id, wired as loaded.

A round at a time (R): ▶ fires every active pair (gold wire) at once, and what a round makes waits for the next, as the lattice's agents would all act together. The bar then has one column a round, its height how many rewrites fired together, its colour what most of them did. Untick it and ▶ is one rewrite.

Every rewrite is an event that keeps the agents it used up and, for each of their ports, where it now leads. Undoing it removes what it made and puts those agents back.

An event can be undone while everything it made is still there: nothing later has used it. Those events are the receipts, the squares beside what they made. Click one to undo it; that can uncover the receipts of the events that made what came back. Click two agents about to meet (gold wire) to fire them.

Events that do not depend on each other undo in any order (tick "undo in any order"). The bar under the net is the history: one column an event, its colour what kind; undoing out of order leaves gaps.

Nothing is lost, so erasers may erase. Taking a value apart is events too, and undoing them builds it again; at the end the net is just the answer. Keeping erased values whole instead stores about as much (fewer events, but the residue stays in the net).

Seams (small rings) mark a wire that a rewrite made by joining two wires, as a pair coming apart does: what later uses that wire depends on the event. Un-copy: an eraser that reaches a duplicator's copy turns the duplicator into a seam, and the value is never copied. It is what keeps the history small: without it, 1 + 1 takes 11 times the events.
